How the ranges are built

Past daily returns are rescaled to today's volatility and resampled in one month blocks, thousands of times.
Each range was checked walk forward: forecasts made with only the data available at the time, scored against what actually happened. The hit rates shown come from about 600 stocks while they were in the S&P 500, 1996 to 2026, so they include crashes like 2008.
There is no price target here. A point forecast model was tested and did not beat a simple average, so it is not shown. Past prices only, no dividends, not financial advice.

Investing basics

Short lessons on the ideas behind long term investing. Open any one to read it. General education only, not financial advice.
Foundations Risk Growing money Costs and accounts Habits
Foundations
1What you own when you buy a stockA share is a small piece of a real business.

A share of stock is part ownership of a company. If the company earns more over time, each share usually becomes worth more, and some companies also pay part of their profit to owners as dividends.

In the short run, a stock's price moves with news, interest rates and mood. Over many years it tends to follow the company's earnings. That gap explains why prices can swing a lot even when nothing about the business has changed.

Buying a stock means betting on a business, not a ticker symbol.
2ETFs and index fundsOne purchase, hundreds of companies.

A fund pools money from many investors and buys a basket of investments. An index fund simply copies a list, such as the S&P 500 (about 500 large US companies), instead of paying a manager to pick winners.

An ETF (exchange traded fund) is a fund that trades on the stock market like a single stock. Many ETFs are index funds. Broad ones such as total market funds spread your money across thousands of companies for a very small yearly fee.

Not every ETF is broad or cheap. Some hold one narrow industry, use leverage to double or triple daily moves, or use options to pay high income. Read what a fund actually holds before buying it.

A low cost broad index fund is the simplest way to own the whole market.
Risk
3Risk and return travel togetherHigher expected returns come with bigger swings.

Cash in a savings account rarely loses value, but it grows slowly. Bonds usually move more than cash and less than stocks. Stocks have grown the most over long periods, and they are also the most likely to drop sharply along the way.

Large US stocks have returned roughly 10% a year before inflation over the last century, but that is an average. Plenty of single years were down 20% or more, and plenty were up 30%.

The extra return from stocks is payment for sitting through the drops.
4Volatility and drawdownsHow far, and how often, prices fall.

Volatility measures how much a price bounces around. A drawdown is the fall from a peak to a low. The S&P 500 fell about 57% from 2007 to 2009, about 34% in a few weeks in 2020, and about 25% in 2022.

Losses and gains are not symmetric. After a 50% drop you need a 100% gain just to get back to even. That is why avoiding very large losses matters more than catching every last bit of upside.

DropGain needed to recover
10%11%
25%33%
50%100%
80%400%
Plan for a big drop before it happens, so you are not deciding in a panic. The Ranges tab shows how wide a stock's swings have been.
5DiversificationDo not let one company decide your future.

Any single company can fail, get disrupted or lose most of its value, even famous ones. Holding many companies across different industries and countries means one bad outcome hurts a little instead of a lot.

Diversification does not stop the whole market from falling together. In a crash most stocks drop at the same time. It protects you from the risk of one company, not from market risk.

Owning ten tech stocks is not very diversified, because they tend to move together. The Holdings tab simulates your stocks falling at the same time for exactly this reason.

Spread out across companies and sectors, and keep any single stock to a small share of the total.
Growing money
6CompoundingReturns that earn their own returns.

When gains stay invested, next year's growth applies to a bigger amount. The effect is small at first and large later, which is why time matters more than almost anything else.

$10,000 growing 7% a yearValue
After 10 years$19,672
After 20 years$38,697
After 30 years$76,123

A quick shortcut is the rule of 72: divide 72 by the yearly return to estimate how many years it takes money to double. At 7% that is about 10 years.

Starting early does more than picking the perfect investment.
7Investing a little every monthDollar cost averaging and time in the market.

Dollar cost averaging means investing a fixed amount on a schedule, such as every payday, no matter what the market is doing. You buy more shares when prices are low and fewer when they are high, and you never have to guess the right moment.

Investing $300 a month for 30 years at 7% a year adds up to about $366,000, from $108,000 of your own money. The rest is compounding.

Trying to jump out before drops and back in before rallies usually backfires. Many of the best days in the market come right after the worst ones, and missing a handful of them cuts long term returns sharply.

Time in the market beats timing the market.
8Dividends and total returnA high yield is not free money.

A dividend is cash a company or fund pays to its owners. Yield is the past year's payments divided by today's price. When a dividend is paid, the share price drops by about the same amount, so the payout is moved from the investment into your account rather than created from nothing.

What matters is total return: price change plus dividends. A fund yielding 12% whose price falls 8% a year has only returned about 4%. Many very high yield funds sell options to generate income, which caps how much they gain when markets rise.

In a taxable account, dividends are usually taxed in the year you receive them, even if you reinvest them.

Judge an income fund by total return, not by its yield alone.
Costs and accounts
9Fees add upSmall yearly costs, big long term difference.

Funds charge a yearly fee called the expense ratio. Broad index funds can cost 0.03% to 0.10% a year. Some actively managed funds and advisors charge 1% or more.

$100,000 for 30 yearsEnding value
7% a year, almost no fees$761,226
7% a year minus a 1% fee$574,349

That 1% fee costs about $187,000 over 30 years. Frequent trading and short term gains taxes take a bite in the same way.

You cannot control returns, but you can control costs.
10Account types in the USWhere you invest changes what you keep after tax.
401(k) or 403(b)
A retirement plan through your employer. Contributions come out of your paycheck, often before tax, and many employers add a match. A match is part of your pay, so it is worth getting in full when you can.
Traditional IRA
An individual retirement account. Contributions may be tax deductible, the money grows untaxed, and withdrawals in retirement are taxed as income.
Roth IRA or Roth 401(k)
You contribute money that has already been taxed. It grows tax free, and qualified withdrawals in retirement are also tax free. Roth IRAs have income limits.
HSA
A health savings account, available with a high deductible health plan. Money goes in before tax, grows untaxed, and comes out tax free for medical costs.
Taxable brokerage
No limits and no withdrawal rules, but you pay tax on dividends and on gains when you sell. Gains on investments held longer than a year are usually taxed at a lower rate than short term gains.

Retirement accounts generally charge a penalty for withdrawals before age 59½, with some exceptions. Contribution limits change every year, so check the current numbers on irs.gov.

Use tax advantaged accounts first, then a regular brokerage account for the rest.
Habits
11Before you investBuild the base first.

Money you will need within a few years does not belong in stocks, because a drop could hit right when you need it. Most guides suggest keeping an emergency fund of three to six months of expenses in cash first.

Paying off high interest debt, such as a credit card charging 20% or more, is a guaranteed return that stocks cannot match.

Invest money you can leave alone for at least five years.
12Common mistakesMost losses come from behavior, not bad luck.
  • Chasing what just went up. Last year's top performer is often not next year's.
  • Selling in a panic. Selling after a crash locks in the loss and often misses the recovery.
  • Too much in one stock. This includes your employer's stock, which ties your savings to your paycheck.
  • Leverage and options without a plan. They can lose more, faster, than the stock itself.
  • Trusting price targets. No one reliably predicts exact prices. Think in ranges of outcomes instead.
  • Checking too often. Daily moves are mostly noise and push you toward bad trades.
A simple plan you stick with beats a clever plan you abandon.
